# Second Witness — Brief

## Summary
Second Witness shows the volunteers who run the Global Meteor Network which of their cameras are working alone and which ones the network depends on. It opens on the cameras that detect meteors every night but that no other camera confirms, so none of their meteors becomes an orbit. From there a coordinator can take any camera down and see who loses their only partner and how many orbits a month disappear, or see which installed neighbour could turn its camera to pair with a lonely one. For any camera it says which other cameras its owner should talk to and why, with a message ready to paste on GMN's mailing list, so volunteers who share a sky can find each other. The network is kept month by month for a year, so a camera's page shows when it lost its partner and a country's shows whether it is getting better. Every answer is a Cypher query on an embedded Relate graph that runs in a browser tab, and a coding agent can ask the same over MCP.

## Problem frame
A meteor only gets an orbit when two or more stations see it, so a GMN camera is worth what its partners are worth. More witnesses also make a better orbit: in October 2026 the median velocity uncertainty was 0.311 km/s with two stations, 32% lower with three and 69% lower with five or more. Coordinators write every year that cameras are still waiting for partners and that cameras stop reporting, but nothing shows the network as a network. GMN's own Status page answers a geometric question per point of the map (how many fields of view cover this spot) and flags inactive cameras. It does not show which stations actually confirm each other, which ones run every night with nobody to confirm them, which one is the only partner of another, or what a region loses when one goes dark. Today operators plan overlap by comparing KML files by hand, and a decommissioned camera's cost to its neighbours is invisible until their orbit counts drop.

## Discovery
- D1. Coordinators state the pain in GMN's annual reports, year after year: "Many sites and participants are still waiting to find partners to improve the coverage on their cameras", and "some lonely cameras in Queensland and New South Wales wait for multi-station partners" — https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/02/06/global-meteor-network-report-2025/ · https://www.emeteornews.net/2023/02/11/global-meteor-network-report-2022/
- D2. Cameras drop out without anyone seeing who it hurts: in 2025, 96 cameras that reported in 2024 stopped, and in Russia "14 of the formerly active cameras were decommissioned" — https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/02/06/global-meteor-network-report-2025/
- D3. Overlap is planned by hand today: operators are told to "point new cameras in function of optimal coverage with other cameras" and to "compare kml files at the same elevation (e.g. 100km)" — https://www.emeteornews.net/2024/02/06/global-meteor-network-report-2023/
- D4. The existing tool is geometric and per point: Status v2's experimental FOV Analysis finds "points that fall within the selected Fov of at least the minimum number of actve cameras" and flags cameras with no detections for 14 or 30 days. - D5. The population is counted: 1,365 cameras contributed orbits in 2025 (236 new), 1,621 cameras ever, about 40 countries, 966,063 orbits that year — https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/02/06/global-meteor-network-report-2025/
- D6. The data is open and refreshed every 6 hours: "The data are released under the CC BY 4.0 license", with a request to cite the site and two papers (Vida et al. 2020, 2021). Each trajectory row lists its "Participating stations" — https://globalmeteornetwork.org/data/
- D7. Nobody else in the contest works on meteors or astronomy: 57 gallery entries and 143 Relate repos read on 2026-11-21 — https://relate.devpost.com/project-gallery (competitor survey kept outside the repo until judging ends)
- D8. GMN has no live API, CLI or MCP for network data: its Datasette platform answered 503 on 2026-11-21 and the `gmn-python-api` package depends on it. What it does publish are static files with open CORS: the location and aim of 1,904 stations, and a per-country, per-night index of which stations uploaded and how many detections each had — https://globalmeteornetwork.org/data/kml_fov/GMN_station_coordinates_public.json · https://globalmeteornetwork.org/status/Rmsindexes/country_dataset.json
- D9. Nobody tracks how many of a station's detections never become an orbit: asked about it on the mailing list in July 2026, a GMN and UKMON developer answered it is "not a statistic that is tracked by GMN as far as I know", and that UKMON's own conversion rate is about 54% — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/16152
- D10. Operators ask for partners on the list: in September 2026 a new operator in Saskatoon wrote that they would be "essentially a lone camera" and asked for networking tips; the advice was a second camera about 200 km away aimed at the same sky. Another operator found his station "very lonely on the map" — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/16294 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/16295 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/12754
- D11. The tools volunteers built are geometric too: UKMON's coverage maps overlay at most three cameras' fields of view, meteorview.net layers stations and their fields at 100 km, the Status Viewer shows fields at several heights and, since November 2024, past days; none lists measured pairs. A 2025 thread asked how to tell which camera to fix first — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/15534 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/15538 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/12017 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/14763
- D12. GMN marks a camera "Decommissioned" after 6 months without uploads; Second Witness does not replace that status, it separates a camera that stopped this month from one that runs with no partner — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/12012
- D13. A coordinator already asked the take-down question: among the uses listed for the Status Viewer is "working out if camera failures are going to prevent trajectories from solving" when the failed camera sits in a poorly served area — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/12013
- D14. GMN's founder named the mailing list as the place to pair cameras: people optimizing their overlap should "send an e-mail to the group" to coordinate orientation — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/90
- D15. Operators want to know when cameras stop and why detections do not pair: a 2024 feature request to alert owners of a missing upload ran to 71 messages, and an operator with five cameras asked how 3 × 150 detections on a clear night gave only 3 triangulations — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/11345 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/10568
- D16. Geometry and measured pairs tell different stories: in January 2026 a new Spanish operator was told "Spain is fairly well covered" and could point in any direction, while 94% of Spain's October 2026 orbits rest on exactly two stations (AE9). Both can be true: the sky is covered, but most meteors there have only one confirming witness — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/15536
- D17. GMN is not the only meteor network, but it is the only open, worldwide one that says which stations saw each orbit and refreshes every few hours. CAMS keeps detailed orbits "under embargo" until validated, per its published notes, and released about 111,000 orbits from 2010–2013 as a spreadsheet. EDMOND merges 15 European networks into a yearly CSV (v6.01, up to 2024-12-31). FRIPON's first data release (2024) covers only fireballs seen by six or more cameras, for non-commercial use. UKMON's cameras already report into GMN under UK codes. Visual-report networks and meteorite catalogues answer other questions — https://www.emeteornews.net/cams/11-cams-orbits/ · https://meteornews.net/edmond/ · https://fireball.fripon.org/ · https://explore.globalmeteornetwork.org/gmn_data_store/participating_station/4965409

## Impact
- **Persona**: a GMN national coordinator, an operator deciding where the next camera in their country goes, or the volunteer behind a camera that runs with no partner and does not know whose camera could pair with it, like the coordinators who list "lonely cameras" by region in each annual report (D1) and the Saskatoon operator who asked the list how to stop being a lone camera (D10); `sin contactar`
- **Pierde hoy**: "a camera in my region went quiet and I only noticed when my neighbour's orbits dropped": cameras that film meteors every night for nothing, orbits that nobody can confirm, and new cameras pointed by guesswork. Coordinators ask whether a failed camera will stop trajectories from solving (D13), and operators ask why their detections do not pair (D15)
- **La cifra**: in October 2026, 1,431 stations uploaded data and 115 of them detected meteors but shared none with another station, so none of their detections became an orbit; ES000C uploaded all 31 nights with 1,797 detections and zero orbits. Between September and October 67 stations stopped appearing; in September they had been one of the two witnesses of 1,228 orbits, and 629 neighbours lost meteors they used to share with them (NZ002C lost 408). Of the 126,593 orbits of October, 62,267 (49.2%) rest on exactly two stations; HU000B is the second witness of 899. The network is 18 islands that never confirm each other, and one station, US004L, is the only link between 86 stations and the rest of North America. Measured with `research/measure.py`; the figures are in `research/metrics-202610.json`
- **Despues del evento**: offer the monthly country report to the GMN coordinators on their mailing list, as a page they can open without installing anything

## Safety
- **Accion irreversible**: `sin accion irreversible: Second Witness only reads GMN's public data and its own committed store; it writes nothing to GMN and contacts no operator; the operator posts the introduction, Second Witness sends nothing`

## Demo surface
- **Que ejecuta el jurado**: on the public page, sees the cameras that work alone, picks one, opens its link or types a station code or a country, and presses "Take it down" or "Who could turn"
- **Que resultado nuevo sale**: the stations that lose their only partner, the stations left with a single one, the orbits per month that lose their second witness, or the stations it alone was tying to the rest of the network, each with the path of shared meteors that proves it; for a lonely camera, the nearby installed cameras that could pair with it by turning, marked as an estimate; and for any camera, whom its owner should introduce themselves to, with the message to copy; and how a station or a country changed over the last year
- **Que apuesta demuestra**: the network's real shape is in the orbits, not in the fields of view; Spain looks well covered on the map and still rests 94% on two-station orbits (D16). Two cameras whose fields overlap on a map may never confirm a meteor (weather, aim, clocks), and two that look apart may confirm hundreds. Building the graph from who actually saw the same meteor turns a coverage map into a dependency graph that can say what breaks, and crossing it with what each camera uploaded separates a camera that is off from one that works and nobody confirms
- **Donde**: `sin superficie: not built yet; the PLAN decides the public URL`

## Key decisions
- Global Meteor Network station pairs — chosen over the Backfocus adapter-chain solver, a DarkSky ordinance-precedent finder and an Astronomical League program planner, because GMN's helped group is counted in its own reports, its coordinators state the pain in writing (D1, D2), and the graph comes straight out of its open data. settled: user-approved
- Pairs measured from orbits, not inferred from fields of view — chosen over geometric overlap from the KML files, because that is what GMN's Status already does (D4, D11), because coverage and confirmation diverge (D16), and because a measured pair is the only one that produced an orbit. settled: user-approved
- The take-down question is the product — chosen over "where should I point my new camera", because that answer is a geometric prediction and this one is measured; it reuses the blast-radius pattern already built in hindsight (`knowledge/hackathons/datahub-agent-hackathon/README.md`). settled: user-approved
- Running cameras with no partner as the first screen — chosen over opening on the take-down, because it is the finding a judge understands without knowing meteors, and the one a coordinator can act on today. settled: user-approved
- "Who could turn" — chosen over "where to put a new camera", because turning an installed camera costs nothing and uses the aim GMN already publishes; it stays marked as an estimate because it is geometry, not a measured pair. settled: user-approved
- Twelve calendar months, one slice per month, the latest as the default view — chosen over two months and over a single day, because a trend shows when a camera was left alone and whether a country is improving, comparing consecutive months still shows who stopped, and a day leaves stations unpaired by weather (71 single-partner stations in the one-day file downloaded on 2026-11-21, against 28 in all of October 2026). Each month stays its own slice, so a whole year never hides a camera that already went dark. settled: user-approved
- Sources are the monthly trajectories, the station coordinates and the per-night upload index, loaded into the committed store — chosen over GMN's Datasette platform, which was down (D8), and over reading GMN live from the browser, because the demo must not depend on GMN being up. settled: user-approved
- The questions are Cypher queries on the store — chosen over computing them in code and keeping Relate as storage, because the criterion measures how the product uses the graph; the scripts in `research/` only produced the evidence for this brief and are not part of the system. settled: user-approved
- No LLM inside the product; the agent is the user's, over MCP — chosen over a built-in assistant, because the answers are deterministic and a model adds nothing a number does not already say. settled: user-approved
- Runs in the browser on Relate's WebAssembly build, with the same store usable from the CLI and MCP — chosen over a hosted server, because judges must run it "without asking you anything" and the DataHub entry did best when it could be checked without installing anything (`knowledge/hackathons/datahub-agent-hackathon/README.md`). settled: user-approved
- Introductions by station code, posted by the operator — chosen over an opt-in contact directory, because GMN publishes no operator identity, a directory needs a server and personal data (against R5 and Safety), and GMN's mailing list is where its people already meet and where its founder asked them to coordinate (D14). settled: user-approved
- The country view is the country report — chosen over a separate report page, because both showed the same queries. settled: user-approved
- GMN as the only source — chosen over CAMS, EDMOND and FRIPON, because only GMN publishes, openly and every few hours, which stations saw each orbit (D17); UKMON's cameras are already in it. settled: user-approved
- Live Cypher for every question, with islands, cut points, same-site groups, moved codes and "could pair" precomputed by the loader into the store — chosen over all-live Cypher, because Relate 1.2.0 has no graph algorithms and a cut-point query that excludes a station ran over 10 min, while take-down, chain and history run in 2–40 ms (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`, S2–S6). settled: user-approved
- The witness chain uses Relate's `shortestPath` — confirmed on October 2026: DE0001 to HU000B in 5 stations, 30 ms; a Spanish island station to Germany returns no chain (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`, S3). settled: user-approved
- Name: Second Witness — a meteor needs a second witness to get an orbit. settled: user-approved

## Scope boundaries
### Deferred
- Where to put a new camera — needs an estimate of the pairs a camera that does not exist yet would make; "Who could turn" is the base it would build on
- More than 12 months — an older month is one more slice, but the demo does not need it
- Other networks (EDMOND's yearly CSV, FRIPON's releases) — another format and a yearly cadence; a pair is a pair, so they could feed the same graph later
### Outside this product
- Orbit quality, meteor showers and any science on the meteors themselves — those are properties of the trajectory, not of who saw it; the orbit uncertainty is used only to weigh a third witness
- Changing anything in GMN or in a station's software — Second Witness reads public data; acting on its answer is the coordinator's call
- Telling a cloudy night from a broken lens — both upload zero detections, so the index cannot hold that difference
- Storing operators' names or contacts, or sending messages — GMN publishes stations by code only; the operator decides whether to post the introduction

## Assumptions
- GMN's monthly trajectory file keeps its column layout, with "Participating stations" as the last column. If it changes, the build command fails loudly instead of building a wrong graph. This holds for the 12 months loaded; GMN served the November 2025 monthly file and the Spanish per-night index of 2024-11-15 and 2025-11-15 on 2026-11-23.
- The store keeps, per month, each pair's shared-meteor count, a few trajectory IDs per pair as proof (R3) and per-station counts (nights, detections, orbits, two-station orbits), not the trajectory rows (about 100 MB a month). A synthetic year of that is 33.2 MB as a `.rl` (13.2 MB without trajectory IDs), under the 50 MB budget (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`, S5).
- The fourth field of each row in the per-night upload index is the number of detections that night (ES0001 shows 0 and ES0005 shows 118 on 2026-10-20). GMN does not document it. When a station uploads more than one folder in a night, the folder with the most detections counts.
- A station's position and aim are its latest entry in the coordinates file dated on or before the last day of the month.
- Station codes are not fixed: an old code can be reused at a new site, and one camera can alternate two codes by season (FR000U from November to March, BE000J from April to October) — https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/14811 · https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main/message/14955. A station that stopped appearing is marked "possibly moved" instead of "stopped" when a code that started that month sits within a few metres of it; the distance is fixed with the same-site threshold in the architecture spike.
- "Who could turn" crosses the two fields of view at 100 km, the height GMN recommends for comparing coverage (D3). It is an estimate, not a pair that produced an orbit.
- Two-station trajectories are a fair proxy for "orbits that depend on a station": a third station might have seen the meteor without making the solution, and that is not counted.
- A cut point measures connection, not lost orbits: the 86 stations behind US004L keep confirming each other. A country's share of two-station orbits also reflects how many cameras it has and how they are aimed, not only fragility.
- Coordinators would use a per-station take-down answer. Nobody at GMN has been asked; the mailing list (https://globalmeteornetwork.groups.io/g/main) is where to ask, and its archive is public; the questions are drafted in `docs/outreach.md`.
- Cameras at the same site are not independent witnesses: RMS keeps two co-located cameras in a solution only when a third camera elsewhere also saw the meteor (D9's thread). Second Witness does not count a camera at the same site as a partner; the distance that makes two cameras one site comes from the coordinates and is fixed in the architecture spike. The 115 running cameras with no partner are unaffected, since a co-located pair with no third witness produces no orbit.
